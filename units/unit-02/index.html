01 / Why design matters

What you will learn

Imagine a student visiting a university library website to check today's opening hours. The information exists, but it is buried under several unfamiliar menu headings. The site may look attractive, yet it has failed that visitor. Good web design helps people find, understand and use information with as little unnecessary effort as possible.

Web design combines planning, organisation, appearance and interaction. It begins by understanding the people who will use a website and the tasks they need to complete. HTML and CSS, introduced in later units, are tools for implementing that plan.

After completing Unit 2, you should be able to:

  1. Explain the difference between attractive design and usable design.
  2. Identify target users and their most important website tasks.
  3. Organise information into understandable pages and navigation labels.
  4. Apply basic principles of visual hierarchy, contrast, typography, spacing and consistency.
  5. Describe responsive design and essential accessibility practices.
  6. Draw a simple homepage wireframe and evaluate it using a usability checklist.
Connection to your profession: a library website supports discovery and access to information; a records-centre website supports clear procedures, authorised access and trustworthy service information. Both need thoughtful, user-centred design.

02 / Start with people

Know your audience and their tasks

User-centred design means making decisions based on the needs, abilities and contexts of the people who will use the website. A website is successful when it enables users to achieve their goals, not simply when it satisfies the designer's personal taste.

Begin with three questions: Who will use the website? What do they want to accomplish? Under what conditions will they use it? A visitor may be using a small phone, slow mobile data, a screen reader or a shared computer.

UserLikely taskHelpful design response
First-year library userFind opening hours and borrowing rulesShow hours clearly and use a familiar "Borrowing" menu label
ResearcherAccess a catalogue or digital repositoryProminent search and clear "Catalogue" / "Repository" links
Records requesterUnderstand how to request an authorised recordShort procedure, required documents and a clear contact route
Registry staff memberLocate retention-schedule guidanceDistinct staff resources and descriptive document titles
Example: "Our website has a modern blue background" describes appearance. "A visitor can locate the library's opening hours within one or two obvious choices" describes a user-focused result.
03 / Design quality

What makes a website easy to use?

Usability concerns how effectively and efficiently people can use an interface and how satisfactory that experience is. A usable website offers a clear route to common tasks, communicates in familiar language and gives visitors feedback about where they are.

Good experience

  • Headings match the visitor's question.
  • Important services are visible without searching extensively.
  • Navigation is consistent on every page.
  • Links and buttons describe what they do.
  • Pages remain understandable on a small screen.

Frustrating experience

  • Important details are hidden in unrelated sections.
  • Menus use unexplained abbreviations.
  • Every page has a different layout.
  • Buttons say "Click here" without context.
  • Small text and crowded blocks slow reading.

Consistency reduces the amount users need to relearn. If the main navigation appears at the top of one page, visitors should not need to hunt for it in a different location on the next page. Consistency does not mean every page must contain exactly the same information; it means similar things should behave similarly.

04 / Information structure

Organise content before decorating it

Information architecture is the organisation and naming of website content so that users can predict where to find things. A sitemap shows the main pages and their relationships. Navigation is the set of links visitors use to move among those pages.

LIBRARY HOME
CatalogueSearch resources
ServicesBorrowing guidance
Opening HoursWhen to visit
ContactGet assistance

A simple four-page sitemap: start with the information visitors are most likely to seek.

Good menu labels use words that visitors understand. "Catalogue" is usually clearer than "Knowledge Discovery Interface" for a basic library menu. "Request a Record" is often clearer than "Client Retrieval Transactions" for a public records service.

Useful structures include a homepage for orientation, a small number of main sections and individual pages for specific subjects. Avoid placing every detail on the homepage or creating many empty pages.

05 / Visual communication

Visual hierarchy, whitespace, typography and colour

Visual hierarchy guides attention towards the most important information first. Size, position, contrast and spacing communicate priorities. A clear page title is more prominent than supporting text; a key search action is easier to notice than a secondary link.

UNIVERSITY LIBRARYFind books and journalsSearch the catalogue or explore library services.Search the catalogue
Notice the reading order: service identity → main task → explanation → action.

Whitespace is the intentional empty space between headings, paragraphs, controls and sections. It helps users see groups of related information; it is not wasted space. Typography means choosing typefaces, sizes, line lengths and line spacing that make text readable. Body text must remain legible on a phone and should not depend on tiny lettering.

Colour can reinforce meaning and institutional identity, but colour alone should not communicate important information. For example, an unavailable service can be labelled "Temporarily unavailable" rather than indicated only with a red symbol. Contrast is the visible difference between text and its background. Low-contrast text is difficult to read, particularly outdoors or for visitors with low vision.

Low contrast

Opening hours: Mon–Fri, 08:00–18:00

This pale text is difficult to read.
Clear contrast

Opening hours: Mon–Fri, 08:00–18:00

This darker text is easier to read.

Use limited, coordinated colours and consistent heading levels. Bold text can emphasise a short phrase, but long paragraphs in capitals or bold become tiring to read.

06 / Actions and feedback

Links, buttons, forms and useful feedback

A link takes the visitor to another page, document or location; a button triggers an action, such as opening a menu or submitting a form. The visible wording should make the result clear. "Download borrowing guidelines (PDF)" describes a destination much better than "Click here".

Forms should ask only for information necessary for the task. Labels such as "Email address" should remain visible, even after the user begins typing. Required fields and errors should be explained in words, not by colour alone.

Weak request form

Enter value 1 and Enter value 2. Visitors cannot tell what information is required.

Improved request form

Record reference number
Example: REC-2026-014
Reason for request
Explain the document you need and your authorisation.

Important: a clear form does not automatically make the service secure. Real record requests require appropriate access controls, privacy safeguards and backend processing. Here, the designs are fictional learning examples.
07 / Different users and devices

Responsive design and accessibility

Responsive web design means creating layouts that adjust to different screen sizes and viewing conditions. On a large screen, a homepage may show several service cards beside each other; on a phone, those cards may stack vertically. Users should not need to zoom sideways to read the content.

Wide screen
Small screen

Responsive layouts change arrangement, not the purpose of the content.

Accessibility means designing so that people with differing abilities can perceive, understand and operate the website. Some users navigate with a keyboard; others rely on screen readers, magnification or captions. These design choices also improve usability for many other visitors.

  • Use meaningful headings and understandable link names.
  • Provide text alternatives for informative images.
  • Keep sufficient contrast between text and background.
  • Allow keyboard access to controls and visible focus indicators.
  • Do not use colour as the only way of conveying meaning.
  • Use plain language and clear form labels.

For example, an image of an archive's opening hours needs an accessible text equivalent. A decorative image can be ignored by assistive technologies. Accessibility is part of good design from the beginning, rather than a feature to add at the end.

Further reading: W3C: Introduction to Web Accessibility; MDN: Responsive Design.

08 / Plan before coding

Wireframes: the blueprint of a webpage

A wireframe is a simple sketch showing where major webpage elements will appear. It focuses on structure and user tasks, not final colours or photographs. A wireframe can be drawn on paper before writing any HTML.

A typical information-service homepage includes a website name, navigation, main heading, prominent service or search area, supporting information and footer. The order depends on users' priorities. There is no universal layout suitable for every institution.

09 / Applied examples

Library and records-management design decisions

Library track

University library homepage

Primary users: students, staff and researchers.

Top tasks: search catalogue, check hours, view borrowing rules, contact a librarian.

Design choice: make search and opening hours immediately visible; label resources in everyday language.

Potential problem: displaying a long list of every service before the catalogue search makes the main task harder.

Records track

Public records-centre homepage

Primary users: staff, authorised requesters and public visitors.

Top tasks: learn retrieval procedures, check service hours, understand required authorisation, obtain contact details.

Design choice: distinguish public guidance from restricted internal systems.

Potential problem: a public button that exposes identifiable record lists or personal information.

A good design decision is supported by a clear reason. For example: "The catalogue search appears near the top because finding resources is a frequent student task." This is more useful than saying, "The search box looks nice here."

11 / Guided practice

Practical: sketch a useful homepage

This activity works with pen and paper or a simple drawing tool. No programming is required.

  1. Choose one of the two scenarios: university library or records centre.
  2. Write down two likely visitors and two tasks they need to complete.
  3. Sketch a homepage with a title, navigation, main task, supporting information and footer.
  4. Use clear, short labels for the menu and the most important action.
  5. Check your wireframe: what is the first thing a visitor notices, and why?

Example: a records-centre sketch could show "Requesting a Record" prominently, with "Eligibility", "Steps", "Service Hours" and "Contact" as supporting links. It should not include real records or personal data.

14 / Key ideas

Unit 2 summary

  • Begin with people and tasks, not colours and decoration.
  • Usability means helping visitors complete tasks clearly and efficiently.
  • Information architecture and navigation make information predictable to find.
  • Visual hierarchy, readable typography, spacing and adequate contrast improve understanding.
  • Responsive and accessible designs work for more visitors and devices.
  • A wireframe helps plan a homepage before building it in HTML and CSS.
  • Information-service websites must consider privacy, especially for restricted records.
